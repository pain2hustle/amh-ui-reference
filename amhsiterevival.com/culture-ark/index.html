      Gangnam Style

      South Korea and global online circulation; 2012 · East Asia

      Gangnam Style is a Korean-language pop song and music video by PSY, released on YouTube on 15 July 2012. Its exaggerated movement and comic performance made a filmed dance something viewers could recognize, repeat and circulate. YouTube records that it became the first music video on its service to reach one billion views, on 21 December 2012. For this collection, the important object is not simply a hit recording: it is the combination of a song, a bodily gesture, a shareable video and a global audience. The official upload is linked rather than copied, so future readers can distinguish the original performance from later imitations and commentary.

      Why keep it · what to know

      Why future people learn something: it offers a compact lesson in how language, visual humor and participation could travel together across borders through a privately operated platform.

      Keep in mind: A view count measures platform activity, not unique people or universal approval. One viral work does not represent all Korean culture. The recording, choreography and video are not supplied as reusable assets.

      Michael Jackson’s Thriller short film

      United States; 1983 · North America

      Thriller joins a performed pop song to a dramatic short film, using dance, makeup and special effects rather than presenting a concert alone. The Library of Congress catalogs the 1983 production and identifies John Landis as director, Michael Jackson and Ola Ray as performers, Michael Peters and Jackson as choreographers, and Rod Temperton as the title-song writer. These credits matter: a familiar star image can hide the collaborative labor that makes a filmed performance possible. The catalog also identifies the physical film format. Preserving that information alongside a viewing link helps a future audience understand both the cultural experience and the material object that once carried it.

      Why keep it · what to know

      Why future people learn something: compare what a song communicates through sound with what a film adds through narrative, bodies, costume and effects. The entry also demonstrates why archives retain production credits.

      Keep in mind: This entry concerns the 1983 short film, not the whole album. Its fictional horror can be frightening. Inclusion is not endorsement of every aspect of its makers. No film frames, music or choreography are licensed here.

      Reggae music of Jamaica

      Jamaica, especially Kingston; later twentieth century onward · Caribbean

      Reggae is a Jamaican musical tradition with roots in communities that included marginalized people in western Kingston. UNESCO describes its development through earlier Jamaican styles and wider Caribbean, North American and Latin influences. It documents a progression through ska and rocksteady, alongside later musical exchanges. Reggae carries more than one mood or purpose: social criticism, spiritual expression, affection and pleasure all belong to its cultural life. Its continuation depends on people learning, performing and listening, including in schools and at festivals. This selection preserves a living practice rather than pretending that one famous performer, one record or one sound can stand for the whole tradition.

      Why keep it · what to know

      Why future people learn something: it shows how music can make room for pleasure and political speech at the same time, and how local experiences can become audible far beyond their place of origin.

      Keep in mind: Reggae is not all Jamaican music. Individual recordings retain their own rights. UNESCO recognition in 2018 is a safeguarding milestone, not a date of invention or a certificate granting reuse.

      Tango of Argentina and Uruguay

      Buenos Aires and Montevideo; urban tradition continuing into the present · South America

      Tango brings music, dance and poetry into a shared urban tradition of Argentina and Uruguay. UNESCO locates its formation among popular communities in Buenos Aires and Montevideo, where European migrants, descendants of enslaved Africans and local-born populations lived together. It is therefore better understood as a cultural meeting and continuing practice than as the invention of one person. Social dance settings known as milongas are part of that story, alongside professional performance, teaching and composition. The catalog preserves both countries in the title because a border-only label would obscure the connected Río de la Plata world in which the tradition developed and continues to be renewed.

      Why keep it · what to know

      Why future people learn something: tango can help future readers examine migration, mixed cultural inheritance, urban sociability and the difference between dancing with others and performing for spectators.

      Keep in mind: Do not reduce tango to a single stage pose or treat one choreography as universal. Its histories include African-descended communities. UNESCO inscription in 2009 is not its origin date.

      Gamelan

      Indonesia; centuries-old traditions continuing today · Southeast Asia

      Gamelan names both a traditional Indonesian ensemble and its instruments. UNESCO describes a percussion-centered grouping that may include gongs, gong-chimes, drums and xylophones, as well as strings and bamboo flutes. The music follows learned conventions of tone, structure and rhythm, so a photograph of instruments alone cannot preserve the practice. People must also pass on ways of playing together. UNESCO documentation places gamelan in religious rituals and public events and describes transmission through schools, families and workshops. The selection invites future listeners to encounter ensemble relationships and community learning, rather than treating unfamiliar instruments merely as decorative objects or assuming that every Indonesian ensemble sounds alike.

      Why keep it · what to know

      Why future people learn something: gamelan shows why cultural preservation needs makers, performers, teaching and occasions of use as well as objects. It encourages listening for a group rather than only a solo star.

      Keep in mind: This is an umbrella entry, not a claim that all regional traditions share one repertory or instrument set. Ritual contexts deserve respect. UNESCO inscription in 2021 does not release recordings or photographs for reuse.

      Washoku and Japanese New Year food traditions

      Japan; living food tradition, UNESCO inscription 2013 · East Asia

      Washoku is more than a menu of famous Japanese dishes. It includes the knowledge used to select ingredients, prepare meals, arrange food and share it with others. New Year celebrations make these relationships particularly visible: rice is pounded into cakes, symbolic foods are prepared, and special tableware helps turn eating into a shared occasion. The UNESCO account emphasizes locally available ingredients, including rice, fish, vegetables and wild plants, and the passing of skills through family meals and education. A future reader should look at the arrangement of a table as well as the contents of a bowl. Both can reveal how people connected seasonal change, hospitality, family relationships and ideas about nature.

      Why keep it · what to know

      Why future people learn something: It shows how an everyday necessity could also carry seasonal symbolism and intergenerational teaching.

      Keep in mind: Washoku does not describe every meal eaten in Japan. Regional and household practices differ; heritage recognition is not proof that a tradition is unchanging or that all its foods are sustainable.

      Día de los Muertos in Indigenous communities of Mexico

      Mexico; living annual observances around late October and early November · Latin America

      Día de los Muertos joins remembrance to the practical work of preparing food, decorating spaces and receiving loved ones. In the Indigenous celebrations described by UNESCO, families welcome the temporary return of deceased relatives through offerings, flowers, candles and favourite dishes. Activities link homes and cemeteries, while the seasonal setting also connects remembrance to the maize-growing cycle. The celebration brings Indigenous belief systems into relationship with Catholic feast days introduced during colonial rule. Preserving this practice means preserving differences between communities, not only a familiar image of a decorated skull. A future visitor should ask who is remembered, who prepares the offerings and what a family believes its obligations to the dead include.

      Why keep it · what to know

      Why future people learn something: It reveals grief and memory as shared cultural work rather than only private emotion.

      Keep in mind: This entry concerns diverse Indigenous observances, not a single uniform national ritual. Accounts of origins are debated; do not present the modern festival as an unchanged pre-Hispanic survival.

      Kente weaving

      Ghana; living textile practice, UNESCO inscription 2024 · West Africa

      Kente is a woven textile assembled from narrow strips made on horizontal looms. Its materials may include cotton, silk or rayon, while its colours and designs communicate more than decoration. UNESCO describes named cloths associated with proverbs, sayings and social situations, and choices influenced by the wearer’s circumstances. Learning involves families, apprenticeships, institutions and public cultural events. The cloth therefore records both skilled handwork and systems for passing knowledge between people. A useful future archive would retain a weaver’s explanation alongside a photograph of a finished cloth. Without that explanation, viewers might admire the geometric surface while missing the histories, social relationships and messages that make a particular textile meaningful.

      Why keep it · what to know

      Why future people learn something: It makes clothing legible as communication, skilled labour and community history.

      Keep in mind: Identify the specific weaving community and cloth whenever possible. Do not assign universal meanings to colours or treat a generic printed pattern as equivalent to handwoven kente.

      Yoga as a cultural and philosophical practice

      India and international transmission; ancient roots and living practices, UNESCO inscription 2016 · South Asia

      Yoga belongs in a cultural archive because it connects ideas about the person with disciplined practice and teaching. UNESCO’s account includes bodily postures, meditation, breathing practices and chanting, together with aims of self-realization and liberation. It also records the importance of relationships between teacher and pupil, alongside learning through ashrams, schools, books and newer media. A photograph of a posture captures only a small portion of this heritage. Future readers should also encounter the language, texts and explanations through which practitioners understood what they were doing. This entry documents yoga as cultural expression and philosophical inquiry, rather than offering a routine to follow or making claims about medical treatment.

      Why keep it · what to know

      Why future people learn something: It shows how people joined bodily action, attention, spiritual aspirations and systems of authority in teaching.

      Keep in mind: Yoga encompasses differing traditions and modern adaptations; no single pose represents them all. Historical or spiritual aims are not clinical evidence. This is not exercise instruction or health advice.

      Turkish coffee culture

      Türkiye; living tradition, UNESCO inscription 2013 · West Asia

      Turkish coffee combines a distinctive preparation with ways of spending time together. Finely ground roasted coffee is slowly heated with water, and sometimes sugar, to create a foamy drink served in small cups. The cultural object is not just the liquid: cups, coffee pots, conversation and the obligations of a host form part of the experience. UNESCO describes coffeehouses as places to exchange news and read, and coffee as a feature of engagements, holidays and visits between friends. Some participants interpret the remaining grounds as fortunes. For a future reader, the important question is how a modest drink could organize social time and provide an accepted invitation to talk.

      Why keep it · what to know

      Why future people learn something: It illustrates hospitality and public conversation through an ordinary shared beverage.

      Keep in mind: This is one regional coffee tradition, not a claim of ownership over all coffee preparation. Fortune-telling is described as a cultural practice, not a reliable prediction method.

      Katajjaniq throat singing of Nunavik

      Nunavik, northern Québec, Canada; living oral tradition, Québec designation 2014 · Arctic North America

      Katajjaniq is a Nunavik Inuit vocal practice in which two performers, most often women, face one another and exchange interlocking sounds. It is frequently a friendly game: the sequence can end when one participant laughs or runs out of breath. Vocal patterns may evoke animals, wind, water and other familiar sounds. The Inuit-led Avataq Cultural Institute describes its importance as inherited knowledge and a source of identity across Nunavik’s communities. A future archive should preserve the performers’ names and explanations, not classify the sounds merely as an unusual musical effect. The encounter between singers is central: listening, timing, humour and shared experience are as important as the audible result.

      Why keep it · what to know

      Why future people learn something: It preserves a form where music, play, environmental knowledge and social relationship overlap.

      Keep in mind: This is specifically the Nunavik practice, not every Inuit vocal tradition. Its precise origin is not known. Respect performers’ consent and community protocols; an explanatory link is not permission to sample a recording.

      The capoeira roda

      Brazil; living practice with international communities, UNESCO inscription 2014 · Latin America

      In a capoeira roda, two players move in relationship at the centre of a circle while others sing, clap and play instruments. The activity cannot be fully separated into dance, combat, music or sport: its meaning emerges from their combination. UNESCO describes the master’s responsibility for transmitting knowledge, setting rhythm and maintaining a shared code of conduct. Participants learn by observing and joining the circle, including its songs and instruments as well as movements. For future readers, an isolated photograph of an acrobatic pose would leave out much of the event. The surrounding people, musical leadership and expectations of respect explain how individual skill becomes a communal cultural practice.

      Why keep it · what to know

      Why future people learn something: It reveals embodied teaching and the memory of resistance through an art sustained collectively.

      Keep in mind: Keep its Afro-Brazilian history and memory of oppression visible. Styles and groups differ. This descriptive entry is not a combat or acrobatic training guide.

      The Great Wave off Kanagawa

      Japan, approximately 1830–1832, Edo period · East Asia

      Hokusai’s color woodblock print places a curling sea above small boats, with Mount Fuji appearing far away beneath the wave. It belongs to Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji, so the apparent main subject is also a frame for looking at the mountain. The Metropolitan Museum dates its impression to about 1830–1832 and identifies its medium as ink and color on paper. The image rewards slow looking: the mountain’s compact triangle contrasts with the spreading water, while the people remain small within the composition. Preserve an identified museum impression rather than an uncredited modern redraw, so future readers can distinguish the historical print from the many later pictures inspired by it.

      Why keep it · what to know

      Why future people learn something: It makes landscape, scale and perspective intelligible through a single image, while the print medium invites discussion of art made in multiple impressions rather than one unique painting.

      Keep in mind: Dates and condition vary between impressions. This entry identifies the Met’s JP1847, not every surviving print. Do not confuse an old underlying artwork with automatic permission for every modern reproduction.

      Epic of Gilgamesh

      Ancient Mesopotamia; selected witness is a seventh-century BCE tablet from Nineveh, in present-day Iraq · West Asia

      The Epic of Gilgamesh tells of a ruler of Uruk and his search for immortality. It survives through written witnesses rather than a single original book. One particularly useful object is the Flood Tablet, the eleventh tablet of the epic, preserved from the library of the Assyrian king Ashurbanipal at Nineveh. In it, Gilgamesh meets Utnapishtim, who describes surviving a divinely sent flood. The British Museum’s record explains that versions of the epic circulated across the ancient Near East. For the ark, pair the story with a description of the clay witness: readers should understand both an imagined world and the physical copying that allowed it to outlast its makers.

      Why keep it · what to know

      Why future people learn something: This offers a long comparison for human fears about mortality and remembrance. It also shows that literature can survive through fragmentary objects, translations and repeated scholarly reconstruction.

      Keep in mind: The seventh-century BCE date belongs to this surviving tablet, not the origin of the entire epic. Ancient stories are not literal eyewitness records. Modern translations and museum photographs may remain copyrighted.

      Pather Panchali

      India, 1955; Bengali-language cinema · South Asia

      Pather Panchali is Satyajit Ray’s first film and the beginning of the Apu trilogy. Its rural Bengali family includes the child Apu, his sister Durga, their mother Sarbajaya and the elderly Indir. The film makes their relationships and everyday surroundings central rather than treating ordinary life as background for a spectacle. Criterion identifies it as a 1955 Bengali-language, black-and-white film running 125 minutes, adapted from Bibhutibhusan Banerjee’s novel. Subrata Mitra’s photography and Ravi Shankar’s music are important parts of the work alongside Ray’s direction. Preserve the credits and language information with any viewing reference: film is a collaboration, and a translated title cannot convey everything about its setting.

      Why keep it · what to know

      Why future people learn something: A future viewer can encounter an artistic representation of childhood, family obligations and rural life while comparing how cinema directs attention through time, framing, sound and performance.

      Keep in mind: A fictional film is not a neutral record of all Bengal or India. Its poverty should not become the sole lens through which viewers understand the culture. Viewing availability varies by location; no full film is included.

      Go, also called Weiqi and Baduk

      Originating in ancient China; long-established East Asian and modern global practice · East Asia

      Go is a board-game tradition whose long history links China, Korea and Japan. The Japanese name Go is widely used in English; Weiqi and Baduk are the Chinese and Korean names. The British Go Association describes the game’s precise origins as unknown, separating historical uncertainty from legends that attribute its invention to an emperor. Its account traces the game’s movement through East Asia and later growth in Europe and elsewhere. For a cultural ark, preserve more than a picture of stones on a board: include a clear rules reference, names in several languages and examples of how communities teach and discuss play. A game is a repeatable social activity, not merely its equipment.

      Why keep it · what to know

      Why future people learn something: Go provides a way to experience inherited culture directly by playing. Comparing local names and organized communities reveals how a shared activity can travel without becoming culturally uniform.

      Keep in mind: Do not present a legendary inventor or a precise ancient founding year as established fact. Historical estimates are approximate. Later rulesets and scoring conventions should be documented explicitly if game records are added.

      Great Mosque of Djenné

      Djenné, Mali; present mosque reconstructed in 1906–1907 within a much older urban and religious setting · West Africa

      The Great Mosque belongs to Djenné’s living townscape, not an isolated collection of ruins. UNESCO’s account places the town within trans-Saharan trade and the regional spread of Islam, and identifies the mosque’s 1906–1907 reconstruction as an expression of local religious architecture. Earthen buildings connect its monumental forms with the materials and skilled work of the surrounding settlement. The useful cultural object is therefore both a building and its relationship to a town. Preserve photographs with dates, architectural explanations and local accounts of care, rather than reducing the mosque to an exotic silhouette. Its history also reminds future readers that reconstruction can form part of heritage rather than automatically erase it.

      Why keep it · what to know

      Why future people learn something: It demonstrates that architectural value can lie in earth, skilled upkeep, religious use and a coherent urban setting, not only in expensive permanent materials or isolated masterpieces.

      Keep in mind: Do not date the current mosque to antiquity merely because Djenné has an ancient history. Accounts of the reconstruction’s colonial context differ; avoid an unsupported single-person authorship claim. This is a religious place, not a public stage.

      Taquile textile art

      On Taquile, textile making is not reserved for a detached category of professional artists. The island lies in Lake Titicaca on Peru’s high Andean plateau, and its weaving traditions carry connections to earlier Andean societies. This selection should keep the maker, wearer and setting together: a pattern removed from a garment loses information about how people use it.       Emoji in everyday digital conversation

      Japan-origin mobile communication, late twentieth century onward; global digital culture · Transregional

      Emoji are picture characters used within written communication, including images of faces, food, animals, places and activities. Unicode traces their association with Japanese mobile phones and explains how incompatible carrier systems created obstacles to exchanging messages. Standardization helped characters travel between devices, but the character and its drawn appearance are not identical things. That distinction matters for preservation: an archive should record a message’s date, platform and intended context instead of assuming a tiny symbol always had one meaning. Emoji also differ from older text emoticons such as a smile assembled from punctuation. Together they reveal efforts to bring facial expression, tone and playful ambiguity into brief written exchanges.

      Why keep it · what to know

      Why future people learn something: Future readers can see how ordinary communication combined language, pictures and shared conventions. It is a useful case of everyday culture shaped jointly by users and technical institutions.

      Keep in mind: Emoji are not a universal language with fixed meanings. Platform artwork can carry separate rights; Unicode’s charts do not grant unrestricted commercial reuse of vendor illustrations. Preserve examples with context and verified artwork licenses.

      Māori haka

      Aotearoa New Zealand; continuing Māori performance traditions · Oceania

      Haka are varied Māori performances involving collective movement and voice, not one dance belonging only to a rugby team. The New Zealand Police’s cultural explanation describes forms connected to iwi identities and occasions including welcome, achievement, weddings and funerals. It also notes participation by both women and men and the place of haka within kapa haka performance. A useful ark entry should therefore introduce the category without pretending that one recording represents every form. When selecting a particular example, preserve its name, composer where known, affiliated community, words, translation and occasion. Those details make the performance intelligible as an expression of identity and relationship rather than a sequence of impressive gestures.

      Why keep it · what to know

      Why future people learn something: Haka illustrates how coordinated performance can express welcome, remembrance, identity and political feeling. It challenges the tendency to interpret unfamiliar bodily expression only as aggression or entertainment.

      Keep in mind: Do not flatten haka into a generic 'war dance' or treat all examples as interchangeable. Learn from appropriate Māori custodians before reenacting. Specific compositions, recordings and cultural obligations require their own attribution and reuse assessment.

      Duduk and its music

      Armenia; historical tradition continuing today · Caucasus

      The duduk is an Armenian double-reed wind instrument whose wooden body is commonly made from apricot wood. Its music accompanies songs, dances and occasions including weddings and funerals. UNESCO describes a frequent pairing in which one musician sustains a drone while another shapes a melody and improvises. Circular breathing helps the accompanying player sustain the musical background. Such an arrangement makes cooperation audible: the foreground line depends on another person maintaining continuity. Different instrument lengths support different registers and moods. Keeping the instrument together with its social settings avoids reducing a living musical practice to a vaguely melancholy sound used outside its context.

      Why keep it · what to know

      Why future people learn something: A two-player performance demonstrates how an apparently solo melody can depend on another musician’s less conspicuous labor.

      Keep in mind: Related regional wind instruments should not be collapsed into one national category. The source’s old estimates of decline are not presented as current statistics.

      Guqin and its music

      China; long historical tradition, continuing today · East Asia

      The guqin is a seven-string zither associated with Chinese intellectual and musical history. UNESCO describes an art cultivated in intimate settings by scholars and noblemen, alongside calligraphy, painting and board-game learning. Its sound changes through open strings, stopped strings and harmonics, with sliding and ornamentation adding further expressive detail. A listener expecting only loudness or spectacle could miss the importance of touch and subtle color. This entry preserves the instrument as a practice of attention, not merely a prestigious antique. It also asks future readers to notice that the historical social standing of an art can shape who gets to learn and perform it.

      Why keep it · what to know

      Why future people learn something: Compare different ways of producing one pitch and ask what a culture values when it prizes an intimate musical encounter.

      Keep in mind: Historical elite associations do not define every present-day player. Dated UNESCO performer counts are deliberately omitted.

      Shashmaqom
